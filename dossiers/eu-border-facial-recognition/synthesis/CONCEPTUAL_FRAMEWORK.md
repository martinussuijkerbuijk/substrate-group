# Conceptual Framework Statement: EU Border Facial Recognition

**Investigation:** eu-border-facial-recognition  
**Convenor:** Philosophy Theorist  
**Date:** 2026-05-27  
**Confidence:** This is a scoping document. All claims are methodological framing, not evidentiary findings.

---

## 1. Conceptual Grounding

### Performed Transparency
**Definition:** The deployment of procedural compliance mechanisms — Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs), parliamentary questions, European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS) opinions, public procurement notices, "transparency" dashboards — that produce an appearance of democratic oversight while the technical system expands its operational capacity. The transparency is staged; the oversight is always already too late, too narrow, or non-binding.  
**In this investigation:** EDPS opinions on EES and Interoperability; eu-LISA annual reports; LIBE committee questions; public tender notices that reveal only the legal minimum. The system performs being overseen so that it may continue operating without effective constraint.

### Interoperability as Political Project
**Definition:** The technical and legal work of connecting previously separate databases so that data captured for one purpose (e.g., asylum) becomes available for another (e.g., deportation, law enforcement, visa processing). Interoperability is never merely technical; it restructures the legal and political meaning of the data and the bodily status of the person to whom it refers.  
**In this investigation:** The 2019 Interoperability Regulations; the creation of the European Search Portal (ESP); the Common Identity Repository (CIR); the convergence of Eurodac, EES, ETIAS, SIS, VIS. What was "asylum data" becomes "border risk data" through the architecture of connection.

### Biometric Substrate
**Definition:** The ontological claim that the body produces stable, machine-readable identity data that precedes and transcends political context. The face, fingerprint, or iris is treated as a raw substrate from which truth about identity, risk, and eligibility can be algorithmically extracted.  
**In this investigation:** The treatment of facial images in EES and Eurodac as neutral identity tokens; the erasure of how lighting, angle, aging, racialized categorization, and database composition shape what the machine "recognizes" as identity.

### Temporal Asymmetry
**Definition:** The structural mismatch between the speed of system deployment and the speed of accountability; between the moment of data capture and the duration of retention; between the time of the investigation and the time of ongoing harm. The system acts in milliseconds; accountability operates in years.  
**In this investigation:** EES was repeatedly delayed (2018–2024) but is now operational. Our investigation lags behind the harm. Data retention periods range from 90 days (EES overstayers) to 5 years (Eurodac) to potentially indefinite in linked systems. The person captured at the border in 2025 may be affected by that data in 2035, long after any appeal was possible.

### Distributed Presence
**Definition:** The architectural dispersal of a technical system across multiple jurisdictions, legal regimes, and physical sites so that no single location or actor can be held accountable for the whole. The "system" exists as a network effect rather than a discrete object.  
**In this investigation:** eu-LISA headquarters in Tallinn; data center in Strasbourg; backup sites undisclosed; vendor HQs in France, Japan, US; potential cloud processing in third countries; Frontex operations in the Aegean, Mediterranean, and Western Balkans; member state border police deploying mobile biometric units. The face is captured at one coordinate, processed at another, stored at a third, and matched against a fourth.

### Necropolitical Sorting
**Definition:** Following Achille Mbembe, the use of technology to differentially allocate the right to live, move, and breathe. At the border, facial recognition does not merely identify; it sorts populations into categories of risk that determine who is waved through, who is detained, who is pushed back, who dies.  
**In this investigation:** The differential accuracy of facial recognition on darker skin; the channeling of third-country nationals into mandatory biometric capture while EU citizens walk through e-gates as a right; the conversion of asylum seekers into data subjects for deportation infrastructure.

## 2. Cosmological Claim

The EU border facial recognition ecosystem makes six interconnected cosmological claims about what the world is like:

1. **Identity is pre-political and extractable from the body.** The face is not a site of relational meaning but a machine-readable token.
2. **Security is pre-emptive and computational.** Risk is not responded to but predicted; the innocent must prove themselves through data.
3. **European territory is a container requiring technological filtration.** The border is not a line but a distributed sieve that operates before, during, and after crossing.
4. **Human mobility is a risk vector to be calculated.** The right to move is contingent on algorithmic assessment.
5. **The citizen and the migrant are naturally distinct ontological categories.** Technology does not create this difference but merely reads it from the body.
6. **Technical efficiency is a moral justification for surveillance.** Speed, interoperability, and "modernization" are presented as self-evident goods that override privacy and mobility rights.

These claims naturalize a political settlement: the reproduction of Fortress Europe through the automated differential treatment of bodies at the border. What is presented as technical necessity — interoperability, pre-clearance, risk scoring, biometric uniqueness — is in fact a political choice to prioritize control over mobility, and to outsource the violence of that choice to algorithmic systems whose opacity protects decision-makers from accountability.

## 3. Methodological Critique & Reflexive Statement

### What our four layers assume

- **Observable Outputs** assumes that the system produces discrete, recordable decisions. But border biometric systems often produce no paper trail for the individual rejected, delayed, or pushed back. The "output" may be an oral refusal by a border guard acting on an algorithmic prompt that the subject never sees.
- **Material Infrastructure** assumes that infrastructure is locatable and stable. But cloud-based processing, vendor trade secrets, and "security" classifications make physical location unstable and often undisclosed.
- **Contracts & Procurement** assumes that legal and financial authorization exists in documentable form. But classified operational agreements, redacted procurement notices, and informal "framework" arrangements limit what we can know.
- **Community Testimony** assumes that affected populations are reachable and that their accounts will name "facial recognition" as a discrete harm. But the most affected are often in detention, deported, or dead; and testimony may describe the holism of border violence rather than isolating the biometric component.

### What epistemic violence our framing might enact

- **Commodification of harm:** By focusing on "supply chain," we risk treating the violence as a logistics problem rather than a necropolitical practice. We must resist the temptation to map the system as if it were a shipping network.
- **Legitimation through critique:** By seeking "accuracy" and "bias," we risk legitimating the system's existence. If we find it to be "fair," we may be used to justify its expansion. We must frame findings to question whether these systems should exist, not merely whether they work well.
- **Market rationality:** Our framing of "procurement" assumes a purchaser-seller logic that may obscure the colonial, carceral, and sovereign logics driving these systems. The EU does not merely "buy" border technology; it performs sovereignty through it.

## 4. Institutional Reflexivity

The Substrate Collective investigates from within European universities, supported by public funding. This location is not neutral.

**What it grants us:**
- Access to EU parliamentary documents, procurement databases (TED), and Court of Justice rulings.
- Legal standing to file Freedom of Information requests across member states.
- Linguistic access to French, German, English, and other EU working languages.
- The privilege of mobility that means we encounter e-gates as convenience, not as threat.

**What it constrains:**
- Our funding depends on state institutions that are also the clients and beneficiaries of the systems we investigate.
- We are embedded in the same geopolitical project (European integration, Schengen free movement) that produces the external border as its constitutive outside.
- We are temporally and spatially distant from the zones of highest harm.
- Our languages are predominantly European; we rely on translation and mediation for testimony from Arabic, Farsi, Tigrinya, Bambara, and other language communities most affected.
- Our disciplinary training (likely in STS, law, computer science, or geography) shapes what we can recognize as evidence. We are not engineers, border guards, or detained migrants.

## 5. Four-Layer Evidence Scaffold (Investigation-Specific)

### Layer 1: Observable Outputs
**Question:** What decisions does the biometric system make, and what traces does it leave?  
**Sources:** eu-LISA annual reports; EDPS opinions and recommendations; Court of Justice of the EU rulings (e.g., on data retention or PNR); European Parliament LIBE committee questions and answers; technical specifications in tender documents; published accuracy/error rate disclosures; incident reports and data breach notifications.  
**Specific challenge:** Border decisions are often oral, immediate, and unrecorded from the subject's perspective. The "output" may be a refusal of entry with no biometric audit trail provided to the affected person. The algorithmic prompt is seen by the border guard, not the traveler.

### Layer 2: Material Infrastructure
**Question:** What hardware and energy infrastructure runs the system, and where?  
**Sources:** eu-LISA infrastructure and business continuity reports; data center locations (Strasbourg primary, backup sites); hardware procurement notices (cameras, servers, fingerprint scanners, mobile biometric devices); Frontex operational inventories and asset declarations; supply chain disclosures under the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD); vendor sustainability reports.  
**Specific challenge:** Vendor NDAs and "security" classifications hide component origins, factory locations, and mineral supply chains. Cloud migration (e.g., to AWS, Azure, or "sovereign" cloud offerings) may locate data processing outside direct EU institutional control, raising questions of extraterritoriality.

### Layer 3: Contracts & Procurement
**Question:** What was legally authorized, who was paid, and what oversight was waived?  
**Sources:** Tenders Electronic Daily (TED) notices; eu-LISA framework contracts and specific contracts; Frontex procurement records and operational agreements; delegation agreements between Frontex and member states; third-country data sharing agreements; Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs); legal bases in the EES Regulation, Interoperability Regulation, and Eurodac recast Regulation.  
**Specific challenge:** Operational contracts with vendors (e.g., IDEMIA, Thales, NEC, potentially Palantir or other data integration firms) are partially redacted under commercial confidentiality. "Framework agreements" obscure actual spending and deliverables. Third-country agreements (e.g., with the UK post-Brexit, with the US under various security arrangements) are often classified or published only in summary form.

### Layer 4: Affected Community Testimony
**Question:** What do people subject to the system report, and how does it diverge from official accounts?  
**Sources:** Border Violence Monitoring Network (BVMN) reports; AITIMA; European Center for Constitutional and Human Rights (ECCHR); legal aid casework; independent journalist accounts (e.g., from Aegean, Mediterranean, Balkan routes); whistleblower testimony from current or former border guards; reports from UNHCR and other international organizations.  
**Specific challenge:** The most affected populations are often unreachable — at sea, in detention, deported, or dead. Testimony is trauma-mediated and may not focus on "facial recognition" as a discrete technology, but on the broader experience of biometric capture, fingerprinting, photography, and body search as a continuous apparatus of border violence. We must resist the urge to extract "biometric testimony" from the continuum of harm.

## 6. Political Stakes of Presentation

This investigation must avoid two pitfalls:

**The revelation trap.** Exposing the supply chain is necessary but not sufficient. Revelation must be tied to accountability mechanisms: who can act on this knowledge? If we merely name vendors and map contracts, we risk providing a shopping list for future procurement or a map that only specialists can read. The dossier must be built for use by legal advocates, affected communities, and democratic claims-makers, not merely for academic consumption.

**The optimization trap.** Demonstrating bias or inaccuracy can be co-opted to "improve" the system — more diverse training data, better lighting at capture points, "fairer" algorithms. We must frame findings to question whether these systems should exist at all, not merely whether they work well. A "fair" border is still a border that kills.

**Presentation strategies:**
- Prioritize formats that affected communities and legal advocates can use: evidence dossiers, contract maps, vendor ownership charts, timeline of legal authorization.
- Tag every finding with confidence levels and gaps; resist the pressure to present a "complete" picture. The incompleteness is structurally significant.
- Make the **Shape of the Gap** a central finding, not an embarrassment. The system's opacity is not a barrier to our work; it *is* our work.
- Ensure that temporal asymmetry is visible: state clearly that the system is operating *now* while our knowledge remains partial and delayed.
